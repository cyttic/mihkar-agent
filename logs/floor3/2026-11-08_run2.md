## Question 1

**Question:** "Is developers' trust in AI related to how much of its code they check?"
**Problems in the question:** None that block classification. Hypothesis 1 is directional ("less"), while the question says only "related". This does not block anything.
**Question type:** Knowledge, relationship. Words that show it: "related to". Nothing is manipulated and no cause is claimed.
**Most important goal:** Not stated. The table shows what each strategy gains and pays.
**Constraints given:** access: no company access, but personal networks, LinkedIn and online groups / participants: 30–60 questionnaire answers / time: about 5 weeks of data collection / ethics: committee approval needed

| Strategy | Setting | Gains | Pays (built-in) | Typical methods | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sample study | Neutral | Generalization over actors (A) | Little control over responses, no context, low precision | Questionnaire survey, then correlation analysis | High. Online, fits 30–60 answers and 5 weeks. Needs ethics approval. |
| Field study (**less common alternative**) | Natural | Realism (C): shows how and why developers check AI code | No statistical generalization, less precise measurement | Case study, interviews, observation | Low. You have no access to a company or team. A few interviews with developers from your networks are possible, but then the setting is not natural. |

- **What the less common alternative gives:** the sample study can show that trust and checking go together. It cannot show why. A field study can show the mechanism. [my inference]
- **Accidental limitations to fix in planning:**
  - Recruitment through LinkedIn and groups gives a self-selected, non-representative sample. State this.
  - 30–60 answers give low power for a small correlation. Calculate the needed sample before you start.
  - "Percentage of AI code checked" is self-reported and can be biased. Memory and social desirability both matter.
  - Low response rate.
- **Causality warning:** a sample study cannot show that trust causes less checking. It can show only that they are related. Hypothesis 1 is worded as a relationship, so this fits.
- **Possible combination:** after the questionnaire, run a few interviews (field study methods, or a judgment study with experienced developers). Choose outlier respondents. This compensates for the missing context and for the "why" of the correlation. This is the explanatory sequential pattern.

## Question 2

**Question:** "Do reviewers report a different number of defects when code is labelled as AI-generated?"
**Problems in the question:** The wording is neutral ("different number"). Hypothesis 2 and the design are causal: the label is manipulated and conditions are compared. I classify by the design.
**Question type:** Knowledge, causality. Words that show it: "when code is labelled", with a manipulated label and random assignment.
**Most important goal:** Not stated.
**Constraints given:** access: no company access / participants: 20–40 developers, 20 minutes each, unpaid / time: about 5 weeks / ethics: approval needed, debriefing needed because of non-disclosure

| Strategy | Setting | Gains | Pays (built-in) | Typical methods | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laboratory experiment | Contrived | Precision (B). Random assignment and control let you test cause. | Artificial context, few participants, low realism | Controlled experiment, online task | Medium to high. A 20-minute online task fits. 20–40 people means about 10–20 per group, which is small. |
| Experimental simulation (**less common alternative**) | Contrived, imitating a real situation | Closer to real review work than a lab task, still controlled | Artificial context. Generalizes only to the kind of situation imitated. | Review of a real pull request in a review tool, with a time limit | Medium. Needs a realistic task from public repositories. Takes more preparation time. |

- **Why a field experiment is not offered:** it needs a real team in a real setting, and you have no access. It is therefore not feasible here.
- **What the less common alternative gives:** more realism (C) than the lab task, with a smaller loss of control than a field experiment. It pays with a harder task to prepare and a shorter reach.
- **Terms:** this is an experiment only because you manipulate the label and compare groups. Keep both parts in the method chapter.
- **Accidental limitations to fix in planning:**
  - Only about 10–20 per group. Check power before you start.
  - One code sample only. Results then hold for that code, not for "code" in general. Consider two or three samples.
  - Random assignment must really be random and recorded.
  - Participants may guess the aim. Test this with a short question at the end.
  - Unpaid participants may drop out unevenly between groups.
  - Defect classification by raters is a coding step. Raters can be biased, so check agreement on a sample by hand, even though they are blind to group.
- **Ethics:** non-disclosure is deception. The committee must approve it, and the debriefing must come before any data use.
- **Possible combination:** a sample study of public pull requests where AI use is openly stated, comparing review comments. This adds realism and generalization. It cannot establish causality, and it is confounded because labelled code differs from unlabelled code in other ways. [my inference]

## Question 3

**Question:** "Is AI-written web code more or less accessible than code written by developers?"
**Problems in the question:** The question is two-sided ("more or less"), but Hypothesis 3 is one-sided ("more violations"). This does not block anything. The measurement is automated, so the question is really about violations that an automated checker can detect. Say so in the proposal.
**Question type:** Knowledge, relationship. Words that show it: "more or less accessible than". It compares two kinds of authors on a measure. A claim that AI causes more violations would need a controlled design, see below.
**Most important goal:** Not stated.
**Constraints given:** access: public data allowed, such as public GitHub repositories / participants: 20–40 developers for a 20-minute task / time: about 5 weeks / ethics: approval needed for developers; public data needs a privacy check

| Strategy | Setting | Gains | Pays (built-in) | Typical methods | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Laboratory experiment (tools as actors, same specifications) | Contrived | Precision (B). Same specifications and the same checker. Can support cause if the AI/no-AI condition is controlled. | Low realism. Generalization limited to the specifications used. | Comparing AI assistants and developers on the same form specifications | High for the AI side (cheap, fast, needs no approval). Medium for the developer side (20–40 people, 20 minutes, ethics approval). |
| Sample study | Neutral | Generalization (A) over many real projects | Little control, no context, low precision. Cannot establish causality. | Repository mining, then comparison of violation counts | Medium. Public data fits your access. Time is short. |
| Experimental simulation (**less common alternative**) | Contrived, imitating a real situation | More realism: developers build forms inside a real framework under real-work conditions | Artificial context, generalization limited to the imitated situation | Staged task with a time limit on a real system | Medium. More preparation time. |

- **Decision-table rule applied:** when tools are compared on the same dataset, this is a laboratory experiment. It gains precision and pays in realism and generalization beyond the specifications.
- **Why sample study is listed:** it is the first candidate for a relationship question. It also makes a different trade: many real projects, but no way to know which code is AI-written, and it cannot establish causality.
- **Accidental limitations to fix in planning:**
  - Your design compares AI assistants with developers working without AI. If developers choose their own group, groups differ in other ways. Decide how developers are assigned, or say that you are not randomizing.
  - Fix and report the AI model, version, prompt and settings. Without them the result cannot be repeated.
  - An automated checker finds only some WCAG problems. State this as a measurement limit.
  - A 20-minute task may be unlike real work.
  - Repository mining: AI authorship is hard to identify, and you must not store developers' identifying details, such as usernames.
- **Possible combination:** the laboratory experiment (precision) with a sample study of public repositories (realism and generalization). Alternatively, add a judgment study where accessibility experts check a sample of the forms by hand. This tests whether the automated count matches expert judgment.
